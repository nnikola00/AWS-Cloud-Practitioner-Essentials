## 1. The progression — Secure → Monitor → Audit → Comply

To monitor your AWS solutions effectively you need ways to provide **insights into resource
utilization**, **identify potential issues**, and **facilitate proactive problem resolution**.

The course frames the whole module as a **four-stage progression**:

```
    🔒 SECURE          🔍 MONITOR          ✅ AUDIT           📜 COMPLIANCE
    ─────────          ──────────          ────────           ─────────────
    Protect data,      Continuously        Periodically       Ensure security
    systems, and       observe and         review and         practices and
    infrastructure     analyze system      assess whether     controls meet
    from unauthorized  activity, network   security controls  the requirements
    access, use,       traffic, and        are EFFECTIVE and  of regulations,
    disclosure,        security events     that requirements  industry standards,
    disruption,        to detect threats   are met and        and contractual
    modification, or   or anomalies        policies adhered   obligations
    destruction                            to
       │                   │                   │                    │
       └───────────────────┴───────────────────┴────────────────────┘
              each stage depends on the one before it
```

| Stage | Definition (as the course words it) |
|---|---|
| **Secure** | Protect data, systems, and infrastructure from **unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction** *(that was Module 8)* |
| **Monitor** | **Continuously** observe and analyze **system activity, network traffic, and security events** to detect potential threats or anomalies |
| **Audit** | **Periodically** review and assess the **effectiveness of security controls**, and check that requirements are met and policies and procedures are adhered to |
| **Compliance** | Help ensure security practices and controls **meet the requirements of relevant regulations, industry standards, and contractual obligations** |

**📌 The distinction that matters: Monitor is continuous, Audit is periodic.** Monitoring
watches in real time and tells you *something is happening*. Auditing steps back at intervals
and asks *are our controls actually working?* You need both — continuous monitoring catches
the incident, periodic auditing catches the control that quietly stopped working three months
ago.

### 🧒 ELI5 — looking after a house

- **Secure** = you fit locks on the doors and windows.
- **Monitor** = the alarm and the cameras are **on all the time**, watching for anything odd.
- **Audit** = **once a year** someone walks round and actually *tests* everything: is that
  lock still working? Is the camera pointing at the door or at the sky? Did anyone ever
  replace the battery?
- **Compliance** = the **insurance company's list of rules** you must follow to stay
  insured — and being able to **prove** you followed them.

The audit is the step people skip, and it's the one that finds the camera pointing at the sky.

## 2. Monitoring — what and why

**Monitoring** in the AWS Cloud is the **continuous process of collecting, visualizing, and
tracking the health and performance** of your AWS infrastructure, services, and applications.
The goal: **ensure optimal performance and identify potential issues**.

**Why it matters:** it gives you a way to **continuously observe and analyze system activity,
network traffic, and security events to detect potential threats or anomalies**. Monitoring
and observability are **critical for the security, availability, reliability, and performance**
of cloud-based workloads and data.

**Monitoring is performed using:**

- **Real-time monitoring tools**
- **Log collection and analysis**
- **Dashboards**

💡 **Why monitoring is *more* important in the cloud, not less:** on-premises you could walk
into the data center and look at the blinking lights. In the cloud there's nothing to look
at — the only visibility you have is the visibility you deliberately configure. A resource
you aren't monitoring is a resource you know nothing about.

## 3. Amazon CloudWatch

> *Your notes write it as "Amazon Cloud Watch" — it's one word, **CloudWatch**.*

**CloudWatch monitors your AWS resources and the applications you run on AWS in real time**,
giving you **system-wide visibility into resource utilization, application performance, and
operational health**.

It does more than just monitor — **four features work together**:

| # | Feature | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Metrics** | Collects metrics from **all your AWS resources, applications, and services** running on AWS **and on-premises servers** |
| **2** | **Alarms** | **Define thresholds on metrics** and **send notifications or automatically make changes to resources** |
| **3** | **Dashboards** | **Customizable home pages** in the CloudWatch console to **monitor your resources in a single view** |
| **4** | **Logs** | **Centralize the logs** from all systems, applications, and AWS services you use |

**Benefits:** visualize and analyze your resources, **operate efficiently with automation**,
use an **integrated view**, **proactively monitor**, and gain insights.

**Use case:** monitor and troubleshoot infrastructure.

### 🏢 The course's worked example, as a flow

A retail company monitoring an application on EC2 instances:

```
  1. METRICS   CloudWatch automatically collects metrics
               (e.g. CPU utilization) from the EC2 instances
                        │
                        ▼
  2. LOGS      They configure CloudWatch to collect logs
               on application performance
                        │
                        ▼
  3. ALARMS    An alarm fires when EC2 utilization stays
               too high for an extended period
                        │
                        ▼
  4. ACTION    The alarm triggers automation that SCALES UP
               the number of EC2 instances    ← nobody was paged
                        │
                        ▼
  5. DASHBOARD A custom dashboard visualizes everything in
               one place; logs are analyzed for insights into
               performance issues and application errors
```

💡 **Step 4 is the whole point.** A metric that only produces a graph is passive. An **alarm
wired to an action** is what turns monitoring into automation — and it's exactly the
mechanism behind **EC2 Auto Scaling** from `compute.md`: CloudWatch is the eyes, Auto Scaling
is the hands.

### 🧒 ELI5 — the car dashboard

CloudWatch is the **dashboard in a car**.

- **Metrics** are the **numbers**: speed, fuel, engine temperature. Measured constantly,
  whether or not you look.
- **Alarms** are the **warning lights**. You decide the threshold — "tell me when fuel drops
  below a quarter" — and it lights up on its own. A clever car doesn't just warn you; it
  **does something**, like switching to eco mode.
- **Dashboards** are the **layout of the panel** — everything you care about arranged in one
  place, so you don't check six separate gauges in six places.
- **Logs** are the **journey record**: everywhere you went, every time the engine stuttered.
  You read those *afterwards*, when working out why something went wrong.

## 4. AWS CloudTrail

**CloudTrail tracks user activity and API usage** in the AWS Cloud, **on premises, and even
with other cloud providers**. It provides a **detailed history of API calls**, so you can
**track changes and identify who made them and when** — helping you understand **what actions
were taken on your AWS resources**.

**Benefits:** **auditing**, **security monitoring**, and **operational troubleshooting**. It
also helps you **prove compliance** and **improve your security posture**.

**Use cases:** compliance and auditing, **identifying security incidents**, troubleshooting
operational issues.

### The three features

| Feature | What it does |
|---|---|
| **CloudTrail events** | Capture details about **actions performed in your account** — **API calls, console actions, or other activities**. **Event history** provides a **viewable, searchable, downloadable, and immutable** record of the **past 90 days** of **management events** in a Region. **No charge** for viewing event history |
| **CloudTrail logs** | CloudTrail **delivers events as log files to your Amazon S3 bucket**. Because the logs are **securely stored**, they can be used to **prove compliance** with regulations such as **PCI** and **HIPAA** |
| **CloudTrail Insights** | **Analyzes your normal patterns of API call volume and error rates**, and generates **Insights events** when they **deviate from those normal patterns**. Enable it on trails or event data streams to **detect anomalous behavior and unusual activity** |

**📌 The 90-day detail is exam-relevant.** Event history is **free but only 90 days**. To keep
records **longer than 90 days** — which every compliance regime requires — you must **create a
trail that delivers logs to S3**. Those two things are often confused; the free console view
is not your audit trail.

💡 **"Immutable" is doing real work in that sentence.** An audit log an attacker can edit is
worthless. This is also why the S3 bucket holding CloudTrail logs is typically locked down
hard — often in a separate account, with an explicit `Deny` on deletion (the explicit-deny
mechanism from `Security.md` §3).

### 🧒 ELI5 — the CCTV and the visitor book

If CloudWatch is the car dashboard, **CloudTrail is the visitor book at reception**.

Every single person who comes in writes down **who they are, what time they arrived, and what
they did**. Nobody can tear a page out — the book is **immutable**.

When something goes missing, the dashboard can't help you. It only says *"the temperature
went up."* The **visitor book** tells you **who walked in at 3 a.m. and which room they
opened.**

**CloudTrail Insights** is the receptionist who has read the book for years and notices:
*"Hang on — normally we get 20 visitors a day. Today there were 4,000, and half of them tried
doors that were locked."* Not a rule anyone wrote — a **deviation from normal**.

🛡️ **In practice:** CloudTrail is the single most important log source in an AWS
investigation, and `AssumeRole` events are usually where you start (see `Security.md` §4).
CloudTrail Insights is behavioural anomaly detection on the control plane — a spike in
`AccessDenied` errors is a classic enumeration signature, someone probing to discover what
their stolen credentials can reach.

## 5. CloudWatch vs. CloudTrail vs. Config — the big three

These three get confused more than anything else in the module. They answer **three different
questions**:

| | **Amazon CloudWatch** | **AWS CloudTrail** | **AWS Config** |
|---|---|---|---|
| The question | **"How is it performing?"** | **"Who did what, and when?"** | **"How is it configured, and is that allowed?"** |
| Records | **Metrics, logs, alarms** | **API calls and account activity** | **Resource configuration state over time** |
| Answers | *CPU hit 95% at 14:02* | *Nikola deleted that bucket at 14:01* | *That bucket became public on Tuesday, and that breaks our rule* |
| Category | **Performance monitoring** | **Activity auditing** | **Configuration compliance** |
| Analogy | The **car dashboard** | The **visitor book** | The **building inspection report** |

```
   Something went wrong at 14:00. Which service do you open?

   "The site was slow"          ──────────►  CloudWatch   (metrics, logs)
   "Who changed that setting?"  ──────────►  CloudTrail   (API history)
   "What settings do we have,
    and which break policy?"    ──────────►  AWS Config   (configuration state)
```

💡 **In a real investigation you use all three in sequence:** CloudWatch says *something is
wrong*, Config says *the configuration changed*, CloudTrail says *this identity changed it,
from this IP, at this time*.

## 6. Compliance — AWS Artifact

**Compliance** = your cloud resources and data **adhering to relevant regulations, industry
standards, and internal policies** regarding security and data protection.

**AWS helps you meet compliance goals by:**

1. **Inheriting the latest security controls** that AWS uses on its own infrastructure
2. **Third-party validation** for **thousands of global requirements**
3. **Streamlining and automating** compliance
4. **On-demand compliance reports**

💡 **Point 1 is the underrated one.** When you run on AWS you **inherit** the controls AWS has
already been audited against — physical data center security, hardware disposal, and so on.
You don't have to prove them yourself; you point your auditor at AWS's report. That's the
Shared Responsibility Model showing up as a *compliance* benefit, not just a security one.

### AWS Artifact

**A service providing no-cost, on-demand access to AWS security and compliance reports and
select online agreements.**

**Benefits:** manage at scale, **save time with on-demand access to compliance reports**,
deploy with more confidence.
**Use cases:** manage select online agreements, and **assess third-party security and
compliance**.

**It has two parts:**

| Part | What it's for |
|---|---|
| **AWS Artifact Agreements** | Your company needs to **sign an agreement with AWS** about your use of certain types of information across AWS services. **Review, accept, and manage agreements** for an **individual account** or **all your accounts in AWS Organizations**. Different agreement types address customers subject to specific regulations, such as **HIPAA** |
| **AWS Artifact Reports** | A developer needs to understand **their responsibility for complying with regulatory standards**. Reports come from **third-party auditors** who have **tested and verified that AWS complies** with global, regional, and industry-specific standards. **Kept up to date with the latest releases.** You can provide these artifacts **to your auditors or regulators as evidence of AWS security controls** |

**📌 The direction of each:** **Agreements** are things **you sign with AWS** (you → AWS).
**Reports** are documents **AWS gives you** to hand to your auditors (AWS → you → auditor).

### 🧒 ELI5 — the restaurant's paperwork

You're opening a restaurant in a building you rent.

- **Artifact Reports** are the **landlord's safety certificates** — the fire inspection, the
  wiring check, the gas safety record. **They did the work and got the certificate**; when
  the council asks about the building, you hand over their paperwork instead of re-testing
  the wiring yourself.
- **Artifact Agreements** are the **contracts you sign with the landlord** — "I promise to
  handle food safely in your building." You sign those; they're your commitment.

### AWS Compliance portal

Contains resources to learn about AWS compliance, including **customer compliance stories**
from regulated industries, plus **whitepapers and documentation** on topics such as:

- **AWS answers to key compliance questions**
- **An overview of AWS risk and compliance**
- **An auditing security checklist**

## 7. AWS Config and AWS Audit Manager

Most companies want you to follow **specific configuration guidelines** for your AWS
resources — which means you need a way to **assess and audit the resources you create** to
make sure they meet those rules.

### AWS Config

**A service you use to assess, audit, and evaluate the configurations of your AWS resources.**

**Benefits:** **evaluate configurations against a desired state**, **manage resource
configuration changes**, and **simplify troubleshooting and remediation**.

**Use cases:** **continually audit security monitoring and analysis**, and **streamline
operational troubleshooting and change management**.

💡 **What Config actually does, concretely:** you define rules like *"every S3 bucket must
have encryption enabled"* or *"no security group may allow 0.0.0.0/0 on port 22."* Config
continuously evaluates every resource against those rules, flags **non-compliant** ones, and
keeps a **timeline of how each resource's configuration changed**. Some rules can
**auto-remediate** — Config notices the drift and fixes it.

### AWS Audit Manager

**Continually audits your AWS usage to simplify risk and compliance assessment.** It
**collects evidence and manages audit data**.

**Benefits:** **saves time with automated evidence collection**, **streamlines collaboration
across teams**, and **helps ensure integrity of audits with read-only permissions**.

**Use case:** **automate evidence collection**, continually audit to assess compliance, and
**deploy internal risk assessments**.

### 📌 Config vs. Audit Manager

| | **AWS Config** | **AWS Audit Manager** |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | **Technical configuration state** of resources | **Audit evidence** for a compliance framework |
| Output | Compliant / non-compliant resources, and a change timeline | **A package of evidence** an auditor can read |
| Audience | Engineers | **Auditors and risk teams** |
| Answers | *"Is this bucket configured correctly?"* | *"Can we prove to the auditor that we were compliant all quarter?"* |

**🧒 ELI5:** **Config** is the **rule checker** — it walks round the building with your list
of rules and says *"this window is unlocked, that one's fine."* **Audit Manager** is the
person who **collects all the evidence into a folder for the inspector** — every check, every
date, every photo — so when the inspector arrives you hand over a folder instead of scrambling.

**The gap between them is the whole reason Audit Manager exists.** Knowing you're compliant
and being able to *prove* it on demand are different problems, and the second one used to eat
weeks of engineer time before every audit.

## 8. AWS Organizations

As companies grow, **managing and governing disparate AWS accounts becomes a challenge**.

**AWS Organizations helps you centrally manage and govern your environment as you grow and
scale**, managing **policies for groups of accounts** and **automating account creation**.

**Benefits:** **quickly scale by programmatically creating new AWS accounts** for resources
and teams; **simplify permission management through SCPs**; **manage and optimize costs
across your accounts and resources**.

**Use cases:** automating account creation, providing tools and access for security teams,
**controlling user access to designated services**, and **sharing common resources across
accounts**.

### Key concepts

An **organization** is a **collection of AWS accounts** managed centrally and organized into a
**hierarchical, tree-like structure** with a **root** at the top and **organizational units
(OUs)** nested under it. Each account sits **directly in the root** or **in one of the OUs**.

| # | Concept | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **AWS Organizations** | Used to **consolidate and manage multiple AWS accounts** in a central location. Creating an organization automatically creates a **root** — the **parent container for all accounts** |
| **2** | **Management account** | The **central account that creates and manages the organization**. Responsible for **overall control and governance** |
| **3** | **Organizational unit (OU)** | A **logical grouping of accounts**. OUs can contain **member accounts or nested OUs** |
| **4** | **Service control policies (SCPs)** | Policies that **place restrictions on the AWS services, resources, and individual API actions** that **users and roles in each account can access**. Applied to **OUs or individual member accounts** |
| **5** | **Member account not in an OU** | An account with **unique requirements** that don't overlap with an OU can sit **directly in the organization**, not under any OU. It still gets benefits such as **consolidated billing** |

```
                        ┌──────────────────┐
                        │  MANAGEMENT      │  creates & governs the organization
                        │  ACCOUNT         │
                        └────────┬─────────┘
                                 │
                        ┌────────▼─────────┐
                        │      ROOT        │  ← the parent container
                        └────────┬─────────┘
             ┌───────────────────┼───────────────────┐
             │                   │                   │
      ┌──────▼──────┐     ┌──────▼──────┐    ┌───────▼────────┐
      │  OU: Prod   │     │  OU: Dev    │    │ Member account │
      │  ┌────────┐ │     │  ┌────────┐ │    │  (not in an OU)│
      │  │Account │ │     │  │Account │ │    │  unique needs, │
      │  │Account │ │     │  │Account │ │    │  still gets    │
      │  └────────┘ │     │  └────────┘ │    │  consolidated  │
      │  SCP applies│     │  SCP applies│    │  billing       │
      └─────────────┘     └─────────────┘    └────────────────┘
```

**In designing your organization, consider the business, security, and regulatory needs of
each department** — and use that to decide **which departments group together into OUs**.

### 📌 The SCP nuance the course doesn't state — and the exam tests

**An SCP does not grant permissions. It sets the *maximum* permissions available.**

An account's effective permissions are the **intersection** of its IAM policies and the SCPs
above it:

```
    SCP says:       "this account may use EC2 and S3"        (the ceiling)
    IAM policy says: "Nikola may use S3 and RDS"             (the grant)
                              ▼
    Nikola can actually:      S3 only
        — RDS is blocked by the SCP ceiling, even though IAM allows it
        — EC2 is allowed by the SCP but never granted in IAM
```

So an SCP is a **guardrail**, not a permission. Allowing something in an SCP grants nobody
anything; it merely stops blocking it. You still need IAM to grant.

**🏢 Typical real use:** an SCP on the `Prod` OU denying `s3:DeleteBucket` and
`cloudtrail:StopLogging` **for everyone, including account administrators**. No local admin
in any production account can turn off the audit logging — which is exactly the guardrail an
auditor wants to see.

### 💡 Consolidated billing

Mentioned only in passing in your notes, but it's a headline Organizations benefit and shows
up on exams: all member accounts **roll up into one bill** paid by the **management account**,
and **usage aggregates across accounts** — so volume discounts and Reserved Instance /
Savings Plan benefits apply across the whole organization rather than per account. Separate
accounts, one bill, better pricing.

### 🧒 ELI5 — the school

- **The organization** is the **whole school**.
- **The management account** is the **head teacher** — they set up the school and make the
  rules for everyone.
- **OUs** are the **year groups**. Year 3 has its own rules; Year 6 has different ones.
- **Member accounts** are the **classes** inside each year group.
- **SCPs** are the **school-wide rules**: *"nobody, in any class, may go into the chemistry
  store."* Even a teacher who normally has every key can't override it — that's the point.
  But the rule doesn't *give* anyone a key either; it only says which doors are off limits.
- **Consolidated billing** is the school paying **one electricity bill** for all the
  buildings, and getting a bulk discount, instead of each classroom paying separately.

## 9. Governance — Control Tower, Service Catalog, License Manager

> *Your notes have the heading as "Govenrnance" — typo for **Governance**.*

### AWS Control Tower

**Enforce and manage governance rules for security, operations, and compliance at scale**
across all your organizations and accounts.

**Benefits:** **saves time while providing governance** using **preconfigured controls**, to
quickly set up **multi-account environments**, **automation with built-in governance**, and
**integration of third-party software at scale**.

**Use cases:** **quickly deploy applications and provision compliant AWS accounts**.

**Four components:**

| # | Component | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Dashboard** | **Continuous oversight** of provisioned accounts across the enterprise, with **controls for policy enforcement** and the ability to **detect noncompliant resources** |
| **2** | **Account Factory** | A **configurable account template** that **standardizes the provisioning of new accounts** |
| **3** | **Controls** (a.k.a. **guardrails**) | **High-level rules providing governance for your overall AWS environment** |
| **4** | **Landing zone** | A **well-architected multi-account environment based on security and compliance best practices** — the **enterprise-wide container** holding all the OUs, accounts, users, and resources you want to regulate |

**📌 Control Tower vs. Organizations — how they relate:** **Organizations** gives you the
*structure* (accounts, OUs, SCPs) but you assemble everything yourself. **Control Tower sits
on top of Organizations** and sets up a best-practice multi-account environment for you —
the landing zone, logging, guardrails, and an account factory — **preconfigured**. Think of
Organizations as the raw material and Control Tower as the opinionated starter kit.

**🧒 ELI5:** Organizations gives you **bricks**. Control Tower hands you a **house that's
already built to code**, with the wiring done properly, plus a **machine that stamps out new
rooms to the same standard** (Account Factory) and an **inspector's screen** showing which
rooms have gone off-spec.

### AWS Service Catalog

**Create, share, and organize from a curated catalog of AWS resources.** You can **deploy
baseline networking resources and security tools for new AWS accounts so you govern
consistently**.

**Benefits:** **quick to find and deploy approved, self-service cloud resources**; **stay
agile while improving governance** across multiple accounts.

**Use cases:** **provision resources across AWS accounts**, **apply access controls**, and
**accelerate provisioning of CI/CD pipelines**.

**The problem it solves**, in the course's own framing: *managing employee requests for new
AWS services is time consuming — but you also don't want everyone guessing which services and
settings to use.*

**🧒 ELI5:** Service Catalog is the **school lunch menu**. Without it, everyone wanders into
the kitchen and cooks whatever they fancy — some of it dangerous. With it, there's a **menu of
approved meals**; you pick one and it arrives, made correctly, every time. You get your lunch
in seconds *and* nobody sets the kitchen on fire.

💡 **Why this is a governance service, not a convenience:** the alternative to a catalog isn't
"people wait for approval" — it's **people building it themselves, wrongly**. A self-service
menu of pre-approved, correctly-configured templates is how you get speed and compliance at
the same time instead of trading one for the other.

### AWS License Manager and BYOL

When companies move to the cloud they must decide how to handle **software licenses**. With
the **Bring Your Own License (BYOL)** model, you use **existing licenses purchased directly
from vendors such as Microsoft** on AWS services like **EC2 Dedicated Hosts** and **Amazon
WorkSpaces**. This can mean **significant cost savings** versus buying licenses from AWS, plus
**flexibility and potentially optimized costs**.

**AWS License Manager** **helps you manage your software licenses and fine-tune your licensing
costs**.

**Benefits:** **visibility and control**, **tracking and managing licenses**, **reducing the
risk of noncompliance**.

**Use cases:** **streamline license management**, simplify the **Microsoft License Mobility
through Software Assurance** experience, and **automate the distribution and activation of
software entitlements** across accounts for end users.

Managing licenses is **time consuming, costly, and difficult to enforce**. License Manager
reduces noncompliance risk by **enforcing license usage limits, blocking new launches**, and
other controls.

💡 **This connects back to `compute.md`:** **Dedicated Hosts** exist largely *because* of BYOL
— some vendor licenses are tied to physical sockets and cores, so you need visibility into
the actual hardware. License Manager is how you then keep track of it.

**🧒 ELI5:** License Manager is the **librarian counting the textbooks**. The school bought 30
copies. If 35 children are using one, the school is in trouble at the next inspection. The
librarian **knows how many are out**, and **stops handing out the 31st**.

### The three governance services

| Service | Definition |
|---|---|
| **AWS Control Tower** | Set up and govern a **secure, compliant, multi-account AWS environment based on best practices** |
| **AWS Service Catalog** | **Create, share, and organize** AWS services and resources **from a curated catalog that you define** |
| **AWS License Manager** | **Manage software licenses and fine-tune licensing costs** |

## 10. AWS Health

**AWS Health** is the **go-to data source for events and changes affecting the health of your
AWS Cloud resources**. It notifies you about **service events, planned changes, and account
notifications** to help you **manage and take actions**.

### AWS Health Dashboard

**View account-specific health information and get AWS Health event updates.** You can also
use AWS Health **programmatically via the AWS Health API**, which is **available with AWS
Premium Support**.

**Benefits:** a **data source for events and changes**, **timely and actionable guidance to
remedy issues**, helps **manage service health**, and is **integrated and automated for use
at scale**.

**Use cases:** **view account-specific health information**, **plan for lifecycle events**, or
**troubleshoot an incident**.

**📌 The distinction that matters:** **CloudWatch tells you about *your* resources. AWS Health
tells you about *AWS's* services** — and specifically **how AWS's own events affect your
account**. If an AZ has a networking problem, or your EC2 instance is scheduled for
retirement, or a certificate you use is expiring, that's AWS Health, not CloudWatch.

**🧒 ELI5:** CloudWatch is **your car's dashboard**. AWS Health is the **radio traffic
report** — *"roadworks on your route Tuesday, and a lane is closed this morning."* Nothing is
wrong with your car. Something is happening on the road **that affects you**.

💡 **The most useful part is the "planned changes."** Finding out *in advance* that your
instance will be retired next month, or a service version is being deprecated, is far cheaper
than finding out when it happens.

## 11. Trusted Advisor and IAM Access Analyzer

### AWS Trusted Advisor

Optimizing large-scale deployments **is not a one-time thing** — you must continuously look
for ways to optimize **costs, performance, security, and resilience**. Trusted Advisor
**continuously evaluates your AWS environment using best-practice checks across several
categories**.

> **All AWS Support plans include dozens of Trusted Advisor checks. With Business Support and
> other advanced plans, you get hundreds of checks.**

**Benefits:** **align with AWS best practices**, **prioritize recommendations**, and
**optimize resources at scale**.

**Use cases:** optimize **cost, efficiency, security**, improve **performance**, and **track
service limits**.

💡 **The check categories** (useful to have in your head): **cost optimization**,
**performance**, **security**, **fault tolerance**, **service limits/quotas**, and
**operational excellence**. Notice they mirror the pillars of the Well-Architected
Framework — Trusted Advisor is essentially an automated Well-Architected review.

**🏢 What it actually finds:** idle load balancers you're still paying for, EBS volumes
attached to nothing, over-provisioned instances, S3 buckets with open permissions, MFA missing
on the root account, no Multi-AZ on a production database, and service quotas you're about to
hit. Cheap wins, continuously.

**🧒 ELI5:** Trusted Advisor is the **helpful friend who walks round your house** and says:
*"you left the fridge door open, that window's unlocked, you're paying for a gym you never
go to, and you're nearly out of milk."* Nothing is broken. But all four are worth knowing.

### IAM Access Analyzer

Although Trusted Advisor **does** check security, you may need help with the **fine-grained
permissions** of IAM. **IAM Access Analyzer helps you meet your least privilege goals.**

It provides capabilities to **set, verify, and refine permissions** by **analyzing external
access** and **validating that your policies match your corporate security standards**.

**Benefits:** **refine permissions**, **validate IAM policies**, **meet least privilege
goals**, **automate IAM policy reviews**.

**Use cases:** **set fine-grained permissions**, **verify who can access what**, **remediate
unused access**, and **refine and remove broad access**.

💡 **This closes the loop on `Security.md`.** The principle of least privilege is easy to
state and very hard to achieve, because nobody knows which of the permissions they granted
are actually used. Access Analyzer answers that: it **reads CloudTrail history to see what a
role really called**, and can **generate a tightened policy** containing only that. It also
flags **external access** — resources shared outside your account or organization, which is
how public S3 buckets get caught.

**🧒 ELI5:** Access Analyzer is the **person who checks who has keys to your house.** *"You
gave your neighbour a key three years ago and they've never used it. Your cousin has a key to
every room but only ever goes in the kitchen. And this key — you don't know whose it is, but
it's held by someone outside the family."* Then it **cuts you a smaller keyring** with only
the keys actually needed.

## 13. Quick recap + common exam traps

### Recap

- The progression is **Secure → Monitor → Audit → Comply**. **Monitoring is continuous;
  auditing is periodic.**
- **CloudWatch** = performance. Four features: **metrics, alarms, dashboards, logs**. An alarm
  can **notify or automatically change resources** — that's what powers Auto Scaling.
- **CloudTrail** = activity. **API calls and console actions**, with **90 days of free,
  immutable event history**; deliver **logs to S3** to keep them longer and prove **PCI/HIPAA**
  compliance. **CloudTrail Insights** flags **deviations from normal API patterns**.
- **CloudWatch = how is it performing · CloudTrail = who did what · Config = how is it
  configured.**
- **AWS Artifact** gives **no-cost, on-demand** access to **reports** (third-party audit
  evidence about AWS, for your auditors) and **agreements** (things you sign with AWS, e.g.
  HIPAA).
- **AWS Config** evaluates resource configurations against a **desired state**.
  **Audit Manager** **automates evidence collection** for compliance assessments.
- **AWS Organizations**: a **root**, a **management account**, **OUs**, **member accounts**,
  and **SCPs**. SCPs **restrict** — they set the **maximum** permissions, they don't grant.
  **Consolidated billing** comes free with membership.
- **Governance trio:** **Control Tower** (landing zone, Account Factory, controls/guardrails,
  dashboard), **Service Catalog** (curated, approved resources), **License Manager**
  (software licenses, **BYOL**).
- **AWS Health Dashboard** = events and **planned changes in AWS** affecting your account.
- **Trusted Advisor** = best-practice checks (dozens on all plans, **hundreds on Business+**).
  **IAM Access Analyzer** = fine-grained permission analysis for **least privilege**.

### Traps

| Trap | The truth |
|---|---|
| "CloudWatch shows who deleted the bucket" | **CloudTrail** does. CloudWatch shows **performance**, not identity |
| "CloudTrail monitors CPU and memory" | **CloudWatch** does. CloudTrail records **API calls and account activity** |
| "CloudTrail and Config do the same thing" | **CloudTrail = actions taken** (who called what API). **Config = configuration state** (what a resource looks like now, and how it changed) |
| "CloudTrail keeps my logs forever for free" | **90 days** of free event history. Longer retention means **a trail delivering to S3** |
| "Monitoring and auditing are the same activity" | **Monitoring is continuous**; **auditing is periodic** and assesses whether the **controls themselves** work |
| "AWS Artifact costs money" | **No cost**, on demand |
| "Artifact Reports are reports about my environment" | They're **third-party audit reports about AWS**, for you to give **your** auditors. Evidence about **your** environment comes from **Audit Manager** |
| "An SCP grants permissions" | **It never grants.** It sets the **maximum available** permissions — a guardrail. You still need IAM to grant |
| "An account administrator can override an SCP" | **No** — that's the point. SCPs apply to **all users and roles** in the account, admins included |
| "Every member account must be inside an OU" | An account with unique needs can sit **directly in the organization**, and still gets **consolidated billing** |
| "Control Tower replaces Organizations" | Control Tower **sits on top of** Organizations, preconfiguring a best-practice landing zone, guardrails, and account factory |
| "Service Catalog is a billing or marketplace service" | It's **your own curated catalog** of approved, pre-configured resources for self-service deployment |
| "AWS Health monitors my applications" | It reports on **AWS service events and planned changes affecting your account**. Your applications are **CloudWatch** |
| "Trusted Advisor checks are the same on every plan" | **Dozens** on all Support plans; **hundreds** on **Business** and above |
| "Trusted Advisor covers IAM permissions in detail" | It checks security broadly. **Fine-grained IAM permission analysis is IAM Access Analyzer** |
