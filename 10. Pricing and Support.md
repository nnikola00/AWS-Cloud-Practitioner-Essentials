## 1. The three key concepts of AWS pricing

| Concept | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Pay as you go** | **Adapt to changing business needs** and **reduce the risk of overprovisioning or missing capacity** |
| **Save when you commit** | For certain services, such as compute, **Savings Plans offer savings over On-Demand prices** when you commit to a **1-year or 3-year** plan |
| **Pay less by using more** | **Realize savings as your usage increases.** For some services pricing is **tiered** — the more you use, the less you pay |

💡 **These three are in tension, and that's the point.** Pay-as-you-go gives you flexibility
at the highest unit price. Committing gives you a lower price in exchange for giving up
flexibility. Volume tiering rewards you for scale. **Cost optimization is essentially the art
of choosing the right one per workload** — which is what §8 works through.

### 🧒 ELI5 — the ice cream van

- **Pay as you go** — you buy one ice cream when you want one. Nothing is wasted; you'd never
  buy 50 in case friends turn up. But each one costs full price.
- **Save when you commit** — you tell the van *"I'll buy one every single day for a year"* and
  they give you a big discount. Brilliant if you really do want one daily; a waste if you go
  on holiday for three months.
- **Pay less by using more** — the first 10 ice creams cost £2 each, the next 50 cost £1.50,
  the rest cost £1. Buying more makes each one cheaper.

## 2. The three driving factors of cost

Pricing **varies based on service category or type, configuration, AWS Region, and which
pricing model you choose.** *(Check the **pricing tab** on any service's webpage for its
specific factors.)*

**There are three fundamental drivers of cost: compute, storage, and outbound data transfer.**

### Compute

You pay **by a span of time** — by the hour or by the second. **Unless you've made a
reservation** (where the cost is agreed beforehand), **you pay from the time you launch a
resource until the time you stop the instance.**

### Storage

Pricing **largely depends on how much storage you have provisioned or how much you're using.**

For some options, such as **Amazon S3**, storage cost is **tiered** — so you can optimize
based on **how frequently and quickly you need to access data** *(the storage classes from
`Storage.md`)*.

**The six S3 cost components:**

1. **Storage** pricing
2. **Request and data retrieval** pricing
3. **Data transfer and transfer acceleration** pricing
4. **Data management and analytics** pricing
5. **Replication** pricing
6. The price to process your data with **S3 Object Lambda**

**📌 Notice components 2 and 3.** People budget for storage and forget that **retrieving**
data costs money too. This is exactly why Glacier is cheap to store and charges you to read —
a lifecycle policy that archives data you actually read weekly can *increase* your bill.

### Data transfer

| Direction | Cost |
|---|---|
| **Inbound** (into AWS) | **Usually no charge** |
| **Between AWS services in the same Region** | **Usually no charge** |
| **Outbound** (out to the internet) | **Charged**, aggregated across services at the outbound rate |

**The more data you transfer out, the less you pay per gigabyte.** For data storage and
transfer, you typically **pay per gigabyte**. *(There are some exceptions — verify data
transfer rates before you begin.)*

### 🧒 ELI5 — the three meters

Think of three meters ticking in your house:

- **Compute** is the **electricity meter** — it runs while the machine is switched on and
  stops when you switch it off. Leave a light on in an empty room and you still pay.
- **Storage** is the **rent on the storage unit** — it costs the same whether you open the
  door or not. Bigger unit, bigger bill.
- **Data transfer** is the **postage**. Receiving parcels is free. **Sending them costs
  money**, and the bigger the parcel, the more you pay — though the more you post, the
  cheaper each one gets.

💡 **"Inbound is free, outbound costs"** is the single most surprising line on most people's
first AWS bill. Moving data *in* is free because AWS wants your data there. Moving it *out*
is where the charge lands.

## 3. Worked scenario — the donations application

An organization built an application that **processes and manages online donations**. Each
of the three cost drivers shows up:

| Driver | What they chose | What drives the price |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Compute** | **Over 500 EC2 instance types** to choose from, each with a different quantity of **CPU and memory** — both compute factors. They picked **`t4g.nano`**, the **lowest price that still met their needs** | Instance type (CPU + memory), and how long it runs |
| **2. Storage** | The application requires storage, so they configured the EC2 instance with **Amazon EBS** | **The amount of capacity configured** |
| **3. Data transfer** | The application **transfers data from the EC2 instance to another solution to run analytics** — this counts as **outbound data transfer** | **Capacity**, and **which internet source or AWS Region** the data goes to |

💡 **`t4g.nano` isn't a random choice** — read it with `compute.md` §3: **`t`** = burstable
(cheap, bursts when busy), **`4`** = 4th generation, **`g`** = **AWS Graviton**, which is
typically the best price-performance option. Then `nano` = the smallest size. That name is
four separate cost decisions.

**📌 The lesson in step 1:** they didn't pick the best instance, they picked the **cheapest
one that still met the requirement**. That's right-sizing, and it's the highest-value cost
lever there is.

## 4. AWS pricing and billing services

These are **purpose-built to help you forecast, track, manage, and view your AWS costs** —
and they're genuinely hard to tell apart when you start.

| Service | What it does | Use cases |
|---|---|---|
| **AWS Organizations** | **Centralized management and governance.** Create, group, and manage accounts; apply **security policies at the account level**; and **consolidate billing** across accounts with a **single payment method** | Consolidate multiple accounts into one central organization · Implement organization-wide policies |
| **AWS Billing and Cost Management dashboard** | **Centralizes cost management**, showing **current charges, usage, forecasts, and detailed breakdowns**. Tools to **manage payments, view invoices, set budgets, and consolidate billing** | Visualizations and billing reports of monthly spend · Set up and manage **payment methods** |
| **AWS Budgets** | **Set custom budgets** and **send alerts** when **costs, usage, or Savings Plans / Reserved Instance utilization or coverage exceed defined thresholds** | Alerts when **projected costs exceed** predefined thresholds · **Forecast future expenses** from current usage trends |
| **AWS Cost Explorer** | **Visualize, analyze, and manage costs and usage** with **interactive graphs, reports, and forecasts**. Insights into **spending patterns, trends, and Reserved Instance recommendations** | Analyze **historical spending trends** to find savings · Forecast future costs to budget effectively |
| **AWS Pricing Calculator** | A **web-based planning tool to create estimates**. Input configurations (**instance types, storage options, data transfer volumes**) and receive a **detailed cost breakdown** | **Estimate costs before deployment** · **Compare** costs of different services and configurations |

### 📌 The distinction that actually matters: WHEN each one is used

This is how to keep them straight, and it's how the exam tests them:

```
   BEFORE you build        WHILE running          AFTER the fact
   ────────────────        ─────────────          ──────────────
   PRICING CALCULATOR      BUDGETS                COST EXPLORER
   "What will this         "Tell me if we go      "Where did the money
    cost if I build it?"    over £5,000"           actually go?"
                                  │
                           alerts you in advance,
                           based on a forecast
```

| | **Pricing Calculator** | **AWS Budgets** | **Cost Explorer** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time frame | **Future — hypothetical** | **Future — projected** | **Past — actual** |
| You have spent | **Nothing yet** | Money is being spent now | Money already spent |
| Its job | **Estimate** before you commit | **Alert** when a threshold is crossed | **Analyze** trends and find savings |
| One word | **Estimate** | **Alert** | **Analyze** |

**🧒 ELI5 of the trio:**

- **Pricing Calculator** — working out **before the holiday** how much it'll cost: flights,
  hotel, food. You haven't spent anything yet.
- **AWS Budgets** — telling your bank *"text me if I'm about to go over £1,000 this month."*
  It watches while you spend and **warns you before it's too late**.
- **Cost Explorer** — sitting down **after the holiday** with the bank statement: *"we spent
  how much on taxis?"* Now you know what to do differently next time.

💡 **The Billing and Cost Management dashboard** is the front door that contains most of the
others — current charges, invoices, and payment methods. If a question mentions **paying the
bill** or **invoices**, that's the dashboard, not Cost Explorer.

### 🧒 ELI5 — the phone-a-friend tiers

Imagine you're stuck on homework:

- **Basic** — you get the **textbook and the class forum**. Nobody to phone. Free, and
  everyone gets it.
- **Developer** — you can phone a helper, and they'll **call you back tomorrow**. Fine while

- **Business** — a helper **answers within the hour if the real thing is broken**. This is
  the minimum once it actually matters.
- **Enterprise On-Ramp** — **half an hour**, and a **team who knows your school** checks on
  you occasionally without being asked.
- **Enterprise** — **fifteen minutes**, and **one specific person whose job is knowing you**
  — your name, your homework, your weak spots.

💡 **A TAM (Technical Account Manager) is the real difference at the top tiers**, not just
speed. A TAM is proactive: they review your architecture, warn you before a limit bites, and
coordinate AWS on your behalf during an incident. That's what you're paying for.

## 6. Additional resources for your cloud journey

> *Your note: `<ADD PICTURE2 AS TEXT AND EXPLAIN EACH>` — the five markers, written out.*

**In addition to AWS Support plans, AWS provides access to other teams, resources, and
documentation** you can use to support your cloud journey. Five of them:

### 1. AWS re:Post

**What it is:** AWS's **community question-and-answer service** — the replacement for the old
AWS Forums. You post a question; it's answered by the community and by **AWS-vetted experts**,
with accepted answers marked.

**Why it exists:** most questions aren't unique. Someone has hit your error before, and the
answer is already there. **Free, and included in Basic Support** — it's literally listed in
the Basic tier of the table above.

**🧒 ELI5:** the **class forum** where everyone asks and answers each other's homework
questions, with the teacher occasionally stepping in to confirm the right answer.

### 2. AWS Trust and Safety Center

**What it is:** where you **report abuse** — AWS resources being used for something they
shouldn't be. Spam, malware distribution, port scanning, DDoS traffic, phishing pages, or
copyright violations originating from AWS IP space.

**Why it exists:** AWS is enormous, so some of it inevitably gets used for abuse. The Trust
and Safety team is how that gets reported and shut down. **It works in both directions:** you
report abuse *coming from* AWS, and if someone reports *your* account, this is the team that
contacts you.

**🧒 ELI5:** the **"report a problem" desk**. If someone in the building is doing something
they shouldn't, this is who you tell — and if someone says *you* are, these are the people
who knock on your door.

🛡️ **In practice:** this is the abuse contact you'd use when an investigation traces hostile
traffic — a scanner, a phishing site, a C2 host — back to an AWS IP. Knowing the reporting
path exists is genuinely useful in a SOC.

### 3. AWS Solutions Architects

**What they are:** **AWS technical experts who help you design** your architecture — advising
on which services fit, how to structure a workload, and how to follow the **Well-Architected
Framework**.

**When you get them:** typically through your account team, as part of the sales and
onboarding relationship, and more extensively at higher Support tiers.

**Their job is advice, not implementation.** They help you decide *what to build*.

**🧒 ELI5:** the **architect who draws the plans** for your house. They don't lay bricks —
they make sure the design won't fall over.

### 4. AWS Professional Services

**What it is:** a **paid consulting organization** inside AWS. A global team that works
**alongside your staff and AWS Partners** to actually **deliver** a project — migrations,
large-scale modernization, building a complex workload.

**The difference from Solutions Architects:** SAs **advise**; ProServe **does the work with
you**, on an engagement, for a fee.

**🧒 ELI5:** the **builders you hire** to actually construct the house from the architect's
plans, working next to your own team.

### 5. Self-Support at AWS

**What it is:** everything you can solve **without talking to anybody** — the **AWS
documentation**, **whitepapers**, **technical guides**, **FAQs**, **AWS Knowledge Center**,
the **Well-Architected Framework**, and **AWS Skill Builder / Training and Certification**.

**Why it's listed as a resource:** it's the **first place to look**, it's **free**, and it's
available at every Support tier including Basic. Most answers are already written down.

**🧒 ELI5:** the **instruction manual and the library**. Before you phone anyone, read the
book — the answer is usually in it.

### The five at a glance

| Resource | What it gives you | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **AWS re:Post** | Community + AWS-expert Q&A | **Free** |
| **AWS Trust and Safety Center** | Report abuse (and respond to reports about you) | **Free** |
| **AWS Solutions Architects** | Expert **design advice** | Via account team / Support tier |
| **AWS Professional Services** | A consulting team that **delivers the project with you** | **Paid engagement** |
| **Self-Support** | Docs, whitepapers, Knowledge Center, training | **Free** |

💡 **Advice vs. delivery is the distinction to hold onto:** **Solutions Architects tell you
what to build. Professional Services help you build it.**

## 7. AWS Marketplace and AWS Partner Network

### AWS Marketplace

**A digital catalog with thousands of software listings from independent software vendors.**
Use it to **find, test, and buy software that runs on AWS**. Each listing has **detailed
pricing options and reviews from other AWS customers**.

**What's in it:**

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Software as a service (SaaS)** | **Business applications** such as project management tools · **Marketing tools** such as customer engagement platforms · **Collaboration tools** such as file sharing services |
| **Machine learning and AI** | **Prebuilt models** for image recognition, natural language processing, and more · **ML algorithms** for training custom models |
| **Data and analytics** | **Business intelligence platforms** for visualization and reporting · **Data integration tools** |

You can also **explore by industry and use case**. For example, in **healthcare** you can
review solutions to **protect patient records** or **ML models that analyze a patient's
medical history to predict health risks**.

💡 **The practical value is billing and procurement, not just discovery.** Marketplace
purchases appear on your **existing AWS bill** and can count toward committed spend — which
means no separate vendor contract, no new payment method, and no procurement cycle. That's
often the real reason organizations buy through it.

**🧒 ELI5:** the **app store for AWS**. Instead of building everything yourself, you browse,
read the reviews, and install something that already works — and it goes on the same bill as
everything else.

### AWS Partner Network (APN)

**A global community that uses AWS technologies, programs, expertise, and tools to build
solutions and services for customers.** Together, **partners and AWS provide innovative
solutions, solve technical challenges, and deliver customer value.**

You can **work with AWS Partners** to create or use **specialized solutions tailored to your
business needs**. *The course's example:* a **retail company** hosts their website on AWS,
then works with an **AWS Partner specializing in advanced analytics and machine learning** to
**improve customer personalization** on that site.

**You can also become an AWS Partner.** Benefits include:

| Benefit | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Funding benefits** | Joining APN and participating in specific programs **unlocks funding** to help **build, market, and sell** with AWS |
| **AWS Partner events** | **Webinars, virtual workshops, and in-person learning.** Network with other partners, learn about offerings, and **collaborate with AWS experts** |
| **AWS Partner Training and Certification** | **Partner-centered offerings** from AWS Training and Certification — from certifications to specific services or learning objectives |

**📌 Marketplace vs. APN:** **Marketplace is where you buy *software*.** **APN is where you
find *people* — consulting firms and technology companies** who build and deliver with you.
Products vs. expertise.

## 8. Cost optimization — a worked example

> *Your note: "Provide Example of Cost Optimization — like we have this this and this, and
> then what could be made better."*

Here's a realistic starting state and what you'd actually change. **Figures are illustrative
but the ratios are typical** — a first-pass optimization on an unmanaged account commonly
lands between 30% and 60%.

### The starting state — "AnyCompany Retail", $4,180/month

| # | What they have | Monthly |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **6 × `m5.xlarge` EC2** web servers, **On-Demand**, running **24/7** — average CPU **9%** | $1,180 |
| 2 | **2 × `m5.2xlarge` EC2** dev/test instances, **On-Demand**, running **24/7** | $560 |
| 3 | **`db.m5.large` RDS MySQL**, On-Demand, Single-AZ, **production** | $250 |
| 4 | **4 TB in S3 Standard** — mostly logs and old order exports, **rarely touched** | $94 |
| 5 | **3 TB of `gp2` EBS volumes**, including **900 GB unattached** (old instances deleted) | $300 |
| 6 | **2.2 TB/month outbound data transfer** — images and product pages served straight from EC2 | $198 |
| 7 | **18 months of EBS snapshots**, never pruned | $210 |
| 8 | **2 idle load balancers** from a decommissioned project | $36 |
| 9 | Nightly **batch job**: 20 × `c5.4xlarge` On-Demand for 3 hours | $1,352 |
| | **Total** | **≈ $4,180** |

### What could be made better

| # | The problem | The fix | Service / concept | Saves |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Web servers at **9% CPU** are massively **over-provisioned** | **Right-size** to `m6g.large` (Graviton), and put them behind **Auto Scaling** (min 2, max 8) so capacity follows demand instead of sitting at peak all day | Right-sizing · **EC2 Auto Scaling** · Graviton | **~$830** |
| **2** | Dev/test boxes run **24/7** but are used ~40 hrs/week | **Schedule stop/start** — off evenings and weekends. 168 hrs → 50 hrs is a **70% cut** | Instance scheduling | **~$390** |
| **3** | Production database is **On-Demand** and **Single-AZ** | Commit to a **1-year Reserved Instance** (~40% off). *Also enable **Multi-AZ** — that costs more, but Single-AZ production is a resilience bug, not a saving* | **Reserved Instances** | **~$100** |
| **4** | 4 TB of rarely-read logs sitting in **S3 Standard** | **S3 Lifecycle policy**: → Standard-IA at 30 days → Glacier Flexible Retrieval at 90 → **expire at 400 days**. Most of it ends up costing a tenth as much | **S3 Lifecycle** + storage classes | **~$75** |
| **5** | **900 GB of EBS volumes attached to nothing**, and all volumes are older `gp2` | **Delete the orphans** (Trusted Advisor lists them), then **migrate `gp2` → `gp3`** — ~20% cheaper with better baseline performance | **Trusted Advisor** · gp3 | **~$110** |
| **6** | 2.2 TB/month of images served **directly from EC2** at full outbound rates | Put **CloudFront** in front. Cached content is served from edge locations at **lower per-GB rates**, and the origin serves far less | **Amazon CloudFront** | **~$70** |
| **7** | **18 months** of snapshots nobody ever prunes | **Data Lifecycle Manager**: keep 7 daily, 4 weekly, 12 monthly, delete the rest automatically | **Amazon DLM** | **~$150** |
| **8** | **Two load balancers with no targets**, billed hourly forever | **Delete them.** Trusted Advisor flags idle load balancers by name | **Trusted Advisor** | **$36** |
| **9** | A nightly batch job on **On-Demand** — but it's **interruption-tolerant** and restartable | Run it on **Spot Instances** (up to 90% off), orchestrated by **AWS Batch** | **Spot** · **AWS Batch** | **~$1,080** |
| | | | **New total ≈ $1,340/month** | **≈ $2,840 (68%)** |

### The general method, in order

Notice that the list above follows a sequence, and it's the sequence to use every time:

```
   1. DELETE WHAT'S UNUSED        orphaned volumes, idle load balancers,
      ───────────────────         ancient snapshots, forgotten test stacks
      Pure win. No trade-off whatsoever. Always do this first.

   2. RIGHT-SIZE WHAT'S LEFT      9% CPU on an xlarge is money on fire
      ─────────────────────       Graviton is usually cheaper per unit of work

   3. TURN THINGS OFF             dev/test doesn't need to run at 3 a.m.
      ────────────────            scheduling + Auto Scaling

   4. MOVE DATA TO THE RIGHT TIER S3 Lifecycle, gp2 → gp3
      ──────────────────────────  match storage class to access pattern

   5. COMMIT TO THE STEADY BASE   Savings Plans / Reserved Instances
      ────────────────────────    ONLY after steps 1-4 — otherwise you commit
                                  to a baseline you were about to shrink

   6. USE SPOT FOR THE FLEXIBLE   batch, CI, rendering, anything restartable
      ──────────────────────────
```

**📌 Step 5's ordering is the mistake people actually make.** Buying a 3-year Reserved
Instance for a fleet you haven't right-sized yet **locks in the waste for three years**.
Clean up first, *then* commit to whatever's genuinely steady.

### Which services do the work

| Job | Service |
|---|---|
| Find the waste | **AWS Trusted Advisor** (idle resources, over-provisioning, open permissions) · **Cost Explorer** (where the money goes, RI recommendations) |
| Model the change before making it | **AWS Pricing Calculator** |
| Stop it recurring | **AWS Budgets** (alert when forecast exceeds threshold) · **Cost Allocation Tags** (attribute spend to a team) |
| Automate the cleanup | **Amazon Data Lifecycle Manager** (snapshots) · **S3 Lifecycle** (objects) · **Auto Scaling** (capacity) |

### 🧒 ELI5 — the family bills

Your family's bills are too high. You don't start by switching energy company — you start
by **looking around the house**:

1. **The freezer in the garage is empty and switched on.** Unplug it. *(Orphaned volumes.)*
2. **You're paying for a 4-bedroom house but three rooms are empty.** Move somewhere smaller.
   *(Right-sizing.)*
3. **Every light is on in an empty house all night.** Switch them off. *(Scheduling.)*
4. **Winter coats are in the expensive climate-controlled storage unit.** Move them to the
   loft. *(S3 Lifecycle.)*
5. **Now** sign the 2-year energy deal — because now you're signing up for what you *really*
   use, not what you were wasting. *(Savings Plans.)*
6. **The big annual clear-out can happen whenever there's a free skip**, so take the cheap
   slot nobody wants. *(Spot.)*

Doing #5 before #1 through #4 means you just signed a 2-year contract to power an empty
freezer.

## 9. Quick recap + common exam traps

### Recap

- **Three pricing concepts:** **pay as you go**, **save when you commit** (Savings Plans,
  1 or 3 years), **pay less by using more** (tiered pricing).
- **Three cost drivers:** **compute** (per hour/second, from launch until stop), **storage**
  (provisioned or used; tiered for S3), **outbound data transfer** (inbound and same-Region
  transfer are usually free).
- **S3 has six cost components** — storage, requests/retrieval, transfer, data management and
  analytics, replication, Object Lambda.
- **Billing services by timing:** **Pricing Calculator = estimate before**, **Budgets = alert
  during**, **Cost Explorer = analyze after**. The **Billing and Cost Management dashboard**
  is the front door (charges, invoices, payment methods). **Organizations** gives
  **consolidated billing**.
- **Five Support plans:** **Basic** (free, everyone, docs + re:Post + core Trusted Advisor
  checks) → **Developer** (24h/12h) → **Business** (4h/1h, **first production tier**, full
  Trusted Advisor) → **Enterprise On-Ramp** (30 min, **pool of TAMs**) → **Enterprise**
  (15 min, **designated TAM**, prioritized recommendations).
- **Five additional resources:** **re:Post** (community Q&A), **Trust and Safety Center**
  (report abuse), **Solutions Architects** (design advice), **Professional Services** (paid
  delivery), **Self-Support** (docs, whitepapers, training).
- **Marketplace** = buy **software** that runs on AWS, billed through your AWS account.
  **APN** = the partner **community** — consulting and technology partners; you can join and
  get funding, events, and training.
- **Cost optimization order:** delete unused → right-size → turn off → tier the storage →
  **then** commit → Spot for the interruptible.

### Traps

| Trap | The truth |
|---|---|
| "Data transfer into AWS costs money" | **Inbound is usually free**, as is transfer **between services in the same Region**. **Outbound is what's charged** |
| "Budgets and Cost Explorer do the same thing" | **Budgets alerts you about the future** against a threshold; **Cost Explorer analyzes the past**. Different directions in time |
| "Use Cost Explorer to estimate a new deployment" | Cost Explorer needs **existing usage**. For something not built yet, use the **Pricing Calculator** |
| "You pay for a stopped EC2 instance" | You stop paying for the **instance** when it's stopped — but **you still pay for its EBS volumes** (and Elastic IPs) |
| "Basic Support includes no Trusted Advisor" | It includes the **core checks**. **Business and above** get the **full set** |
| "Developer Support is enough for production" | **Business Support is the recommended minimum for production workloads** |
| "Every paid plan gets a TAM" | **Enterprise On-Ramp** gets a **pool**; only **Enterprise** gets a **designated** TAM. Developer and Business get none |
| "Enterprise Support guarantees a 15-minute fix" | It's a **15-minute response time** for business- and mission-critical systems being down — response, not resolution |
| "AWS Marketplace and the AWS Partner Network are the same" | **Marketplace = software products.** **APN = partner companies and expertise** |
| "Solutions Architects will build it for you" | SAs **advise on design**. **Professional Services** is the paid team that **delivers with you** |
| "Commit to Savings Plans first to save the most" | **Clean up and right-size first.** Committing to an un-optimized baseline locks in the waste for 1–3 years |
| "Archiving everything to Glacier always saves money" | Glacier is cheap to **store** and charges to **retrieve**. Archiving data you read regularly can **raise** the bill |
